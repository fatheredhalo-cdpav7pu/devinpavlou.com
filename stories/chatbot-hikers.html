AI reliability

Chatbots don’t climb mountains. So why are hikers getting lost after trusting them?

By Devin Pavlou Originally published in Straight Arrow News · Oct. 8, 2026

Bryce Vincent Gowryluk was stuck on a ledge on a nearly vertical rock face, several hundred feet up a route he never meant to climb: the Widowmaker, a difficult rock climbing route in the North Shore Mountains north of Vancouver that has killed climbers in the past.

“I am trapped on a ledge,” the 16-year-old recalled saying, according to The New York Times. “There’s nowhere for me to go.”

He had used Anthropic’s artificial intelligence chatbot Claude to help plan for the trip. He packed 20 pounds of climbing gear and water for what he expected to be an eight-hour hike. But as it turns out, using a machine — even partly — to plan mountain excursions could lead to bad outcomes.

Gowryluk is the latest hiker to need rescue after using an AI chatbot to help plan a trip. At least three other cases have been reported since 2025, in British Columbia, California and Poland. Rescuers and researchers say the cases share a problem: A chatbot sounds just as sure of itself when it’s wrong as when it’s right, and people tend to trust confidence.

A pattern of rescues

Gowryluk doesn’t blame Claude. He told The Times he made a navigational mistake and that he also used Google Maps, AllTrails and other tools to plan.

North Shore Rescue responded to Gowryluk’s call that he made while still on the rock face, deploying a rescue team and helicopter to transport him down the mountain.

Paul Markey, a search manager with the team, told Straight Arrow that Claude had suggested Crown Couloir, a scramble over loose rock. Gowryluk, who climbs at a high level in indoor climbing gyms but was on his first outdoor climb, went farther around the mountain and ended up on Widowmaker Arête, a multi-pitch rock climb that requires gear and partners.

“Any fall from the ledge that he was on would have resulted in, at the very least, significant injury, if not death,” Markey said. “You can’t just blindly follow what Claude is suggesting to you,” he added. “Claude’s never been in that area.”

It was the first AI-related rescue for Markey’s team, but other rescuers in British Columbia had already raised the alarm. In April 2025, Lions Bay Search and Rescue helped two hikers down the Unnecessary Mountain trail after they descended into snowy conditions. The group urged people not to rely on ChatGPT or Google Maps in the backcountry.

“We see an emerging pattern and an increase in AI assisted planning for trips,” Maria Masiar, a training officer and search manager for the group, told Straight Arrow. “It is happening across the corridor and across the world.”

In July, two Lithuanian hikers trying to shorten their route through Poland’s Tatra Mountains followed ChatGPT’s advice onto climbing terrain and rescuers had to evacuate them by helicopter, according to the Polish rescue service TOPR.

The following month, three novice hikers were stranded overnight on California’s Mount Shasta after relying heavily on Google’s Gemini for route information and a packing list, according to the Siskiyou County Sheriff’s Office. Their planned eight-hour ascent took twice as long, and they got lost descending in the dark. The sheriff’s office said Gemini had advised the group to bring far less food and water than it needed.

A Google spokesperson said the company doesn’t have the hikers’ prompts and couldn’t reproduce that advice in its own tests. The spokesperson didn’t know whether Gemini’s tested answers specified how much food and water to bring.

Masiar, who also works as an AI interface designer, said the answer a chatbot gives hinges on who is asking.

“The quality of an AI answer depends partly on the quality of the question,” she said. “The quality of the question depends partly on the user’s expertise.”

“We use certainty to decide who to trust more.”
Celeste Kidd, UC Berkeley

How confidence persuades

Humans depend on one another’s specialized knowledge more than any other animal, and they need a way to decide whose knowledge to rely on, Celeste Kidd, a psychology professor at the University of California, Berkeley, told Straight Arrow.

“We use certainty to decide who to trust more,” Kidd said.

Chatbots have nothing built in that tracks how certain they are, she said, so they produce confident statements whether an answer is true or fabricated. Kidd said people read doubt in pauses and stumbles in speech. Chatbots show none, so they read like confident humans.

Masiar said chatbots are easy to use and often give useful answers, which builds trust.

“Imagine asking it ten questions and receiving eight excellent answers. You quickly learn to trust it,” she said. “The problem is that the two incorrect answers may be presented with exactly the same confidence as the correct ones.”

OpenAI didn’t address the rescues but pointed to its user guidance, which acknowledges ChatGPT can sound confident even when it’s wrong and urges people to verify important information. Anthropic didn’t respond to a request for comment.

Research suggests that people fall for that confidence. A study conducted by researchers at the University of California, Irvine, and published in Nature Machine Intelligence found people generally overestimate the accuracy of chatbot responses.

A chatbot also knows nothing about the person asking, Masiar said. It can describe a trail without establishing whether that trail suits the hiker. Someone who knows the terrain works the other way around.

“A local expert wouldn’t necessarily begin by recommending a route,” she said. “They would first want to understand the person attempting it.”

For Gowryluk, he told The Times that he would think twice before trusting a chatbot on his next adventure.

“I’d still use AI for stuff like business and booking meetings and learning new things,” he told the outlet. “But for trails? No. Never again. Never, never again.”

Why this story matters

Multiple hikers have needed emergency rescue after using AI chatbots to plan backcountry routes, with documented cases showing the tools provided confidently stated but dangerously inaccurate guidance on terrain, gear and supplies.

  1. Chatbots present errors confidentlyAccording to researchers, chatbots have no built-in mechanism to signal uncertainty, so incorrect route or gear advice reads as authoritative as accurate information.
  2. Rescue cases are documentedAt least four AI-assisted hiking rescues have been reported since 2025 across British Columbia, California and Poland, with one involving a helicopter evacuation.
  3. Advice varies by user inputAccording to a search manager who also works as an AI interface designer, the quality of a chatbot's answer depends partly on the expertise of the person asking the question.
