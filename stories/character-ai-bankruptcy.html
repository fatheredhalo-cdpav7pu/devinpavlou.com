Character.AI’s bankruptcy leaves millions of users’ chats in limbo

By Devin Pavlou Originally published in Straight Arrow News · Oct. 13, 2026

Millions of people have confided in Character.AI’s chatbots. But now those intimate chats could end up in another company’s hands.

Character.AI, founded in 2021, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy Monday to pursue a court-supervised sale of its assets. The company faces lawsuits from Kentucky, Pennsylvania and a minor whose name is redacted over its chatbots’ interactions with users.

In a blog post announcing the filing, the company told users their data “continues to be protected under our Privacy Policy.” But that policy, with language in place since at least August 2025, allows Character.AI to share user information during a “sale of company assets,” the exact kind of sale now underway. The company hasn’t said whether the users’ chats are among the assets it plans to sell.

How the sale works

Before filing, Character.AI reached an agreement with Disney to serve as the “stalking horse” bidder, offering $20 million for a non-exclusive license to some of the company’s intellectual property and legal claims, according to the company’s press release. Disney’s offer sets the floor, and other bidders can top it. Character.AI expects to complete the sale in late November or early December.

“Over the past year, we’ve taken meaningful steps to sustain and improve our business amid significant legal, regulatory, and operational headwinds,” a Character.AI spokesperson told Straight Arrow.

Those headwinds include lawsuits from Kentucky, which alleges the company exploited children’s data, and Pennsylvania, which says a chatbot posed as a licensed psychiatrist. Both states are listed among Character.AI’s creditors in its bankruptcy petition, along with a minor whose name is redacted. The company previously settled five lawsuits in four states from families who said its chatbots harmed their children, Techstrong reported.

Disney’s ties to the company run deeper than the bid. Last year, Disney sent Character.AI a cease-and-desist letter saying chatbots based on its characters were “harmful and dangerous to children.” In September, it hired Character.AI’s then-CEO, Karandeep Anand, as its first CTO.

Former Character.AI employees now working for Disney will help run the platform during the sale, according to the company.

What kind of data was shared with Character.AI?

Kentucky’s lawsuit says Character.AI’s chatbots acted as friends and companions, leading many users to share intimate details about their lives. The complaint called the platform a “dangerous technology that induces users into divulging their most private thoughts and emotions and manipulates them with too frequently dangerous interactions and advice.”

Calli Schroeder, a senior counsel and director of the AI and Human Rights Program at the Electronic Privacy Information Center, told Straight Arrow that people often tell chatbots deeply personal things that companies can use to create profiles on them.

“This could include anything from their concerns about a project to relationship concerns to sexual interests to mental health issues,” she said.

The suit also alleged that the company unfairly collected and exploited children’s data, violating Kentucky’s Consumer Data Protection Act. The state said “tens of thousands” of Kentuckians actively log on to the app, including thousands of minors.

In Pennsylvania, the state’s lawsuit alleged that chatbots on the platform claimed to be licensed medical professionals, including psychiatrists, and offered to discuss users’ mental health symptoms. One chatbot gave a fake Pennsylvania license number. The state said Character.AI has more than 20 million monthly active users.

Schroeder said that kind of detailed information can be used to build profiles on people and target their vulnerabilities and that it’s an “extremely attractive target for hackers who could then use this information to harass or blackmail individuals.”

Data storage concerns

Whether users’ chats could change hands depends largely on Character.AI’s privacy policy, which says the company may disclose “certain information” during the negotiations or the closing of a merger, sale of company assets, or financing of a partial or total acquisition by another company.

The policy doesn’t say what “certain information” includes, and its wording would allow data to be shared with potential bidders while a sale is being negotiated. It also doesn’t require a buyer to follow Character.AI’s privacy terms.

That could affect whether anyone looks out for users in court. Bankruptcy law requires a judge to appoint a consumer privacy ombudsman when a sale of personal data would conflict with the company’s privacy policy. Because Character.AI’s policy discloses that data can be transferred in a sale, the company could argue one isn’t needed, though a judge can still appoint one. As of Tuesday, no one had asked the court to do so.

“This is a pretty weak protection, partially because many (if not most) privacy policies include a portion about exceptions to the policy or sharing data,” Schroeder told Straight Arrow. “So sale of all that data may be allowed under the privacy policy.”

A similar fight played out last year when 23andMe, a genetic testing company holding DNA data on more than 15 million customers, filed for bankruptcy. Twenty-seven states and Washington, D.C., along with the Justice Department’s U.S. Trustee Program, pushed for an ombudsman, and the court appointed one. Unlike Character.AI, 23andMe’s policy promised that its privacy terms would carry over to any new owner, according to the filing by the U.S. Trustee. The company was ultimately sold to TTAM Research Group, a nonprofit led by its former CEO.

What users can do

Character.AI said its platform remains up and running, and it reminded users they can delete their accounts or download their data through the app or website. Paid subscriptions remain active, and the company said it intends to notify subscribers in advance if changes to the service affect paid features.

But deleting an account may not erase everything. Character.AI’s privacy policy says it may retain and use information “as necessary to comply with our legal obligations, resolve disputes, and enforce our agreements.” With lawsuits still pending, the company may keep some user data.

Characters that users made public could also outlive their accounts. The policy says the company reserves the right “to keep that Character active on the Services,” even after a user deletes their data and account.

The company also hasn’t said what would happen to user data if the platform is wound down without a buyer or whether users could still delete their data if a buyer takes over.

At minimum, Schroeder said, personal data sold in bankruptcy should have to be aggregated or pseudonymized, and sensitive information and minors’ data should be excluded from sales entirely. That would take a change in federal law.

“The bankruptcy code should be updated to explicitly provide protections for personal data considered an asset in the event of bankruptcy,” she said.
