A chatbot message led to a felony charge. Experts say it may not hold up

By Devin Pavlou Originally published in Straight Arrow News · Oct. 8, 2026

One morning in late September, Carli Heller opened up an artificial intelligence chatbot and began venting. She took aim at the Lee County Sheriff’s Office in Florida, saying she was “going to shoot up the sheriff’s right the f— now,” she wrote to the phone app, according to a probable cause statement. Heller continued venting a day later, writing that it was “100% LAST CHANCE” and that she “GOT A NEW F—— GUN TODAY.”

Anthropic’s safety features triggered and sent the chats to its human review team to analyze. The team believed there was a big enough risk to escalate the risk to the FBI, which then notified the sheriff’s office. Authorities arrested Heller on Sept. 30, charging her with making a written or electronic threat of a mass shooting or act of terrorism, a felony under Florida law to which she pleaded not guilty.

Heller admitted writing the messages but denied any intent to carry them out, the report says. She pleaded not guilty.

Heller’s is at least the third case in the U.S. since August in which messages typed to an Anthropic chatbot ended up with the police, following incidents in San Antonio and San Francisco reported by KSAT and The San Francisco Standard. Two cases, including Heller’s, led to arrests.

The cases raise questions about how private chatbot conversations are, when companies can hand them to police without a warrant, and whether words typed to a machine can be a crime. Eugene Volokh, a First Amendment scholar at the Hoover Institution at Stanford University, told Straight Arrow he doubts they can in this case.

Heller’s arrest

Court records reviewed by Straight Arrow say Heller initially told a detective she didn’t remember the messages before admitting to them, denying any intent to carry them out, the report says. She pleaded not guilty.

According to the court records, she “believed the AI platform was a private space where she could express her thoughts,” describing it as a “personal diary.” She denied owning any firearms, and the arrest form indicates authorities didn’t seize a weapon. Heller said she was upset with the sheriff’s office because deputies were present when her child was removed from her custody under an emergency pickup order, according to the report.

The document says the messages were made on Anthropic’s AI. It also says Heller told the detective the platform she used was called Perplexity, which is a separate company. Heller’s attorney, Scott Moorey, declined to say which she used. Anthropic and Perplexity did not respond to Straight Arrow’s request for comment.

Moorey told Straight Arrow that Heller considered her chats “private and protected.” But there’s just one problem: Users’ chats aren’t private.

“Typing your thoughts into a chatbot may not be as private as you might think.”
John Coleman, Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression

Chatbots don’t keep secrets

A growing share of Americans bring personal questions to chatbots, including medical advice.

Many companies say they use chats to train their models. A 2025 Stanford study of six AI developers found all of them did so by default, and some let people review transcripts. AI companies have also faced lawsuits alleging their chatbots contributed to suicides and violence.

Heller’s arrest report says the AI platform “utilizes safety and security measures designed to monitor for key phrases and potentially threatening content.” Anthropic’s consumer terms reserve the right, at its “sole discretion,” to report information about users, including what they type, to law enforcement.

OpenAI said in a 2025 policy post that conversations in which users appear to be planning to harm others are routed to human reviewers and may be referred to authorities. Everytown, a gun safety advocacy group, released a report in September that found Anthropic, Google and xAI have made no comparable public commitment specific to their chatbots. OpenAI, Google and xAI did not respond to a request for comment.

Police usually need a warrant to seize a person’s chats. But the Stored Communications Act, a law that generally bars tech companies from sharing users’ private information, allows them to alert authorities in an emergency, said F. Mario Trujillo, a senior staff attorney at the Electronic Frontier Foundation.

“This is allowed to happen in only narrow circumstances, and there is always the risk of over-reporting,” Trujillo said.

There are no public numbers on how often AI companies do this. The Lee County Sheriff’s Office said it doesn’t keep a count of tips from Anthropic. Trujillo said it would be a “good idea” for companies to reveal those numbers in their annual transparency reports.

Are chats protected speech?

Moorey said he plans to challenge whether a chatbot message can be a written threat under Florida law.

In the past, Florida courts have required proof that a person meant to threaten or knew the message would be viewed as a threat, according to Volokh, who has taught law for 30 years. He said a jury is unlikely to find that beyond a reasonable doubt.

“I doubt that this is a punishable threat, unless there is evidence that the woman subjectively realized at the time that the message would be communicated to a human being who would perceive it as a threat,” Volokh said.

He said it’s likelier that Heller assumed her words would stay between her and the machine “as most of us do when using a chatbot.” Asked whether a company’s terms of service disclosing that chats can be reviewed would change that, Volokh said the question is what she was thinking when she typed.

“Even if the terms say that’s possible (and if she knows in the abstract that it’s possible), she may well not have been thinking about it at the time,” he said.

John Coleman, legislative counsel for AI and free expression at the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression, agreed. If a person typed threatening words with no expectation that anyone else would receive them, he said, “it’s hard to see how that would fit the legal standard to constitute a true threat.”

The State Attorney’s Office for the 20th Judicial Circuit, which is prosecuting the case, did not immediately return a request for comment. Prosecutors have not filed a formal charge. Heller’s arraignment is scheduled for Nov. 2.

“Typing your thoughts into a chatbot may not be as private as you might think,” Coleman said. “But a company seeing those words doesn’t necessarily transform them into a true threat.”

Why this story matters

AI chatbot conversations are not legally private, and companies can share what users type with law enforcement without a warrant under existing federal law.

  1. Chatbots monitor what users typeAnthropic's terms reserve the right to report user messages, including chat content, to law enforcement at its sole discretion, and its platform uses automated monitoring for threatening language.
  2. No warrant required for tipsUnder an emergency exception in the Stored Communications Act, AI companies can voluntarily hand user chats to police without a court order, according to an Electronic Frontier Foundation attorney.
  3. Legal outcome remains contestedTwo First Amendment legal scholars said it is unclear whether messages typed to a chatbot with no expectation of human review can meet the legal standard for a "true threat" under Florida law.
